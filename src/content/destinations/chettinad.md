Chettinad is a dry, flat stretch of Tamil Nadu between Madurai and Thanjavur, with seventy-five villages and some of the most surprising architecture in India. From the 1850s to the 1930s the Nattukottai Chettiars, a caste of bankers and traders, made fortunes lending money across British Burma, Malaya, Ceylon and French Indochina, and they sent the profits home to build. The result is village after village of mansions the size of city blocks, with teak from Burma, marble from Italy, chandeliers from Belgium, tiles from Birmingham and ironwork from Glasgow, each laid out around a run of courtyards for a family of hundreds.

Then the money stopped. The Second World War and independence closed the Chettiars' overseas businesses, the families moved to Chennai and Singapore, and the mansions were left to caretakers. Of roughly ten thousand houses built, a few thousand survive. Many are locked, and their contents have gone to the Karaikudi antiques market. A handful have been restored as hotels, and sleeping in one is the reason to come. You wake under a twenty-foot ceiling and have breakfast in a courtyard of granite pillars. Outside, the only traffic on a street of palaces is a bicycle and a goat.

The food is the other reason. The traders brought star anise and pepper-heavy masalas back from south-east Asia and added them to what was already Tamil Nadu's most confident meat cooking. Chicken Chettinad is on menus all over India. Here it tastes of roasted spice more than chilli, and it arrives with crab, quail, mutton kola urundai and the small shallow-fried rice cakes called kuzhi paniyaram. Two nights is right. Spend one day on the mansions, the Athangudi tile works and the antiques, and the other on a cooking class and the village shrines with their giant terracotta horses. Then on to Madurai, an hour and a half to the south-west, or Thanjavur, two hours north.
