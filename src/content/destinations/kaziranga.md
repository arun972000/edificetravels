In 1905 there were perhaps a dozen one-horned rhinos left on this stretch of the Brahmaputra floodplain. Today Kaziranga holds around 2,600, more than two-thirds of all the greater one-horned rhinos on earth, and a dozen in a morning is normal. They share 430 square kilometres of tall elephant grass, swamp and riverine forest with wild water buffalo, swamp deer and more than a thousand elephants. The park also has one of the highest densities of tigers anywhere, though the grass is usually too tall to see them. Many of our guests tell us it was their best morning of wildlife in the country.

The park is divided into four ranges, each with its own gate on the highway. Kohora, the Central range, is the busiest and has the largest herds of elephant and buffalo. Bagori, the Western range, is best for rhino, and Agaratoli, in the east, for birds and quiet. Burapahar, at the far western end, is hilly forest where you go for primates. Safaris are in open jeeps, one at dawn and one in the afternoon, and we vary the ranges over two or three days so you see the whole floodplain. The Brahmaputra, several kilometres wide, forms the park's northern edge. A boat on it at sunset, with Gangetic dolphins surfacing, is a good end to a day.

Kaziranga is also the centre of a region most travellers to India never reach. Assam's tea gardens, which grow half the country's tea, line the highway. Majuli island, the heart of Assamese monastic culture, is two hours east, and the hills of Nagaland, Meghalaya and Arunachal Pradesh are each a day's drive. We give the park at least two nights and usually build it into a ten-day journey through the northeast that starts in Kolkata. If you've already seen Rajasthan and Kerala, this is where we'd send you next.
