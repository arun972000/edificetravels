In much of the world, religious buildings are things you visit. In India they are still busy every day. Fifteen thousand people walk through the Meenakshi temple in Madurai on an ordinary Tuesday, the kitchen at the Golden Temple feeds around a hundred thousand people a day, free, and in Varanasi the whole city comes down to the river at dawn to bathe and pray. A spiritual journey here is about being present for that, quietly and with good company.

The north has the great pilgrimage places. Varanasi, the oldest living city on the Ganges, is the one most people remember longest. Amritsar's Golden Temple is calm and generous at four in the morning, when the holy book is carried in to the sound of hymns. Rishikesh, where the river leaves the hills, has the ashrams, and Dharamshala, higher up, has the Dalai Lama's temple and the Tibetan community around it.

The south has the temple towns, where the buildings are larger and the festivals older. The Chola temple at Thanjavur and the Meenakshi temple at Madurai are still at the centre of their cities. And far to the north, beyond the Himalaya, Ladakh's monasteries hold morning prayers with long horns sounding from the roofs.

## How we plan a spiritual journey

We start with timing: the hours of prayer, the festival calendar and the days when a place is too crowded to enjoy. Then the guides, who matter more here than anywhere, because they decide whether you feel like a guest or an onlooker. We brief you on dress and manners before you leave, and we leave space in each day for sitting still.

## Who it suits

Anyone curious about how people live their faith, whether or not they share it. Photographers, too, with the right guide. It sits well alongside the heritage of the Golden Triangle, or as the reason to add Varanasi or Amritsar to any northern route.
