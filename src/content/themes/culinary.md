You could plan a journey through India by its food alone, and some of our guests do. The country's cooking changes as fast as its languages: wheat and dairy in the north, rice and coconut in the south, mustard oil and river fish in Bengal, and in between a hundred regional kitchens that rarely make it onto restaurant menus abroad.

The best of it is cooked at home and sold in the street. Old Delhi's lanes have stalls that have fried the same thing for a century. Amritsar's dhabas make kulcha in clay ovens from early morning, and the Golden Temple's kitchen serves a free meal to everyone who comes. In Chettinad, merchant families cook some of the most peppery food in India in kitchens the size of houses. In Kerala, lunch comes on a banana leaf, and in Kolkata the sweet shops are an argument that never ends.

## How we plan a food journey

We plan around meals as carefully as monuments. That means a family dinner every few days, a morning market with a guide who shops there, a street-food walk at the right hour, and restaurants chosen for the cooking rather than the view. We tell drivers where you'd like to stop for lunch on the road, and we keep a list of places that suit nervous stomachs.

We also talk about staying well, plainly. Most guests eat widely and are fine. A few lose a day. Our food and health guide explains how to tip the odds in your favour.

## Who it suits

Anyone who travels for food, cooks at home, or wants to understand a place through what people eat. It mixes easily with heritage in the north or with slow travel in the south.
