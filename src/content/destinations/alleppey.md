The backwaters are a network of lakes, canals and river mouths that runs along most of the Kerala coast, behind a strip of sand and coconut palm. Alleppey, or Alappuzha in Malayalam, is the town at the centre of it: a former spice port with a lighthouse and a long beach, and now the place the houseboats leave from. South and east lies Kuttanad, the only place in India where rice is grown below sea level, in paddies walled off from the canals by earth bunds and kept dry by pumps. The villages sit on the bunds, a single row of houses with the canal at the front door and the paddy at the back.

The houseboat is the reason most people come, and it's worth doing once. The kettuvallam was a working rice barge, thirty metres of jackwood planks tied together with coir rope and thatched with palm, and in the 1990s someone put a bedroom on one. There are now well over a thousand of them. The good ones have one to three cabins, a cook who makes a proper Kerala lunch, and an owner who doesn't overcrowd the boat. One night is enough. The boats must moor by five in the evening, and the daytime route is on the wide canals, where you share the water with a hundred other barges. It's lovely for a day. On the second day it's the same.

So we plan two nights, each a different way. The first is on a boat. The second is in a village homestay or a small hotel on the lake at Kumarakom, with a dawn canoe trip through the narrow canals the barges can't enter. Those canals are where people actually live. The fish seller comes by boat in the morning, and you'll hear church bells and temple drums from opposite banks. From Alleppey the sea is forty minutes away at Marari, a fishing beach with a few quiet resorts, and most of our Kerala journeys end there or further south at Kovalam.
