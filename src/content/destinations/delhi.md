Most journeys to India begin in Delhi because that's where the flights land, and most travellers plan to leave as fast as they can. We'd ask you for two nights. Delhi has been a capital at least seven times, and each one left a city behind: the Sultanate towers of Mehrauli, the Mughal lanes of Shahjahanabad, the white bungalows and roundabouts of Lutyens' New Delhi, and the glass of Gurgaon beyond the ring road. With a car and an early start, you can cover eight hundred years of it before lunch.

The trick is to split it. Old Delhi is a morning city. There's the Jama Masjid, the fried breakfasts of Paranthe Wali Gali, the silver lane of Dariba Kalan, the spice merchants of Khari Baoli and the Red Fort behind its two kilometres of wall. Go early, on foot, with someone who knows which doorway to duck through, and be out by noon. New Delhi is for the afternoon: Humayun's Tomb in its walled garden, the Lodhi tombs among the joggers, an hour in the National Museum, and a drink at the Imperial as the sun goes down.

Then there's the Delhi visitors rarely see, which we think is the best of it. Parakeets nest in the ruins at Mehrauli. At Hauz Khas, a fourteenth-century madrasa stands over a lake, with cafés in the village next door. At the Sikh gurdwara of Bangla Sahib, volunteers roll chapatis for thousands of people a day and will happily let you join in. And on Thursday nights at the Nizamuddin shrine, qawwali that has been sung there for seven centuries carries across the courtyard long after dark. Two days covers most of this without rushing.
