Jaisalmer is the last town before the Pakistan border, and you feel it. The Thar surrounds it on every side, the light is harder and yellower than elsewhere in Rajasthan, and the fort on its hill is built from the same sandstone as the ground, so at sunset fort and hill turn the same colour. It's five hours from Jodhpur, the nearest city, and the long drive across the desert is part of the experience.

The fort is one of the very few in the world where people still live, which is what makes it special and also what's damaging it. There are three thousand residents, a palace, and seven Jain temples carved between the twelfth and sixteenth centuries as delicately as anything at Mount Abu. There are also a great many guesthouses, whose drains are slowly dissolving the hill. We walk it at seven in the morning, when the lanes belong to the people who live there, then come down into the town for the havelis. The Patwa brothers built five mansions side by side. The Salim Singh haveli has a peacock roof, and Nathmal's was carved by two brothers, one on each side, so the two halves don't quite match.

Then there's the desert, which is why most people come. The dunes at Sam, forty kilometres out, are where the tour buses go, and we drive straight past them. Our camps are small and further out on the open desert, with proper beds, hot water and a fire, and Manganiyar musicians whose families have sung for the desert's Rajput courts for generations. You ride a camel up onto the sand for sunset, eat outside, and wake to a dawn so quiet you can hear the camels chewing. One night in the desert and one in town is the right split. A second night in camp is for people who already know they love it.
