We're an India-based operator, so we have a stake in this question. We've tried to write the guide fairly anyway. The company you choose shapes an India trip more than any hotel does, and an honest comparison costs us nothing.

## Three kinds of company

**Foreign-owned India specialists.** These are based in the UK, the US or Australia, staffed by people who have travelled in India and who talk to you in your own time zone and currency. In the UK they carry ATOL or ABTA protection; in the US, state seller-of-travel registration. The ground work (hotels, drivers, guides) is almost always subcontracted to an Indian operator. You pay a margin for the office and the protection, and the person who designed your trip isn't the person who runs it.

**India-based operators.** Companies like ours, in Delhi, Jaipur, Kochi or Mumbai, that book the hotels, employ or contract the drivers and know the guides. They're usually cheaper for the same hotels, since there's no second office to pay for. Your contact is in India, so a message sent at 6 am gets an answer. The weaknesses are real. Consumer protection is weaker than ATOL, standards vary enormously, and you have to do your own checking.

**Aggregators and marketplaces.** TourRadar, Viator, GetYourGuide and the group-tour platforms. Budget group tours are the cheapest way to see India with a guide, the reviews are pooled and the platform holds your payment. You get a fixed itinerary shared with strangers, and you often don't know who is operating it until you've booked. They're good for a first look at India on a tight budget. They aren't tailor-made.

## What to check before you pay

Check seven things: company registration, a written itemised quote, a named specialist, reply time, the driver and vehicle, the payment terms and independent reviews. Most take a few minutes each.

**Company registration.** Every registered Indian company has a CIN (Corporate Identification Number) on the Ministry of Corporate Affairs register, and a GSTIN for tax. Both can be checked online for free in a minute, so ask for them. A business that can't produce them is a sole trader at best. India's Ministry of Tourism also recognises approved inbound tour operators, and there are trade associations. If a company claims either, ask for the number and look it up.

**An itemised written quote.** Before you pay anything, insist on a written quote that names every hotel and room category, states what is and isn't included, shows whether the 5% GST is inside the total, and sets out the deposit, payment dates and cancellation terms. "Four-star or similar" is not a hotel, and a single total with no breakdown is not a quote. Our guide to [what shapes the cost of a private India journey](/guide/what-a-private-india-trip-costs) shows how to compare quotes line by line.

**A named specialist.** One person, by name, who designs the trip and stays with you through it. If the first email comes from a sales team and the second from someone else, expect the same pattern on the road.

**Reply time.** A good operator replies to a first enquiry within a day and sends a full proposal within a few days. A company that is slow to answer an enquiry will be slow when your flight is cancelled too.

**Driver and vehicle standards.** Ask what car, what year, and whether the same driver stays with you for the whole journey. Tourist vehicles in India carry a yellow commercial number plate and an All India Tourist Permit. Private white-plate cars aren't insured to carry you. For two to four people the standard is a Toyota Innova Crysta or equivalent, with working seat belts in the back.

**Cancellation and payment terms.** Get them in writing before the deposit. A deposit of 20 to 30% to confirm hotels is normal, with the balance due 30 to 60 days before travel. Payment should go to a company bank account or by card, never to an individual's account. Cards give you chargeback rights. Wire transfers don't.

**Reviews on third-party sites.** Read them on Google, TripAdvisor and Trustpilot, because a company's own page shows only what it chooses to. Start with the three-star reviews, which show how problems were handled. Reviews that name staff and specific hotels are more trustworthy than a run of five-star adjectives all posted in the same week.

## Red flags

- A quote far cheaper than every other for the same "private" trip. The gap gets covered by shopping commissions, poor hotels or missing guides.
- Hotels described by star rating or "or similar" instead of by name.
- A single total with no itemised breakdown, or cancellation terms that only arrive after the deposit.
- "Factory visits", "demonstrations" or "handicraft centres" in the itinerary. These are shops that pay the agency.
- Pressure to pay before a discount expires.
- Requests for payment by Western Union, to a personal account or in cryptocurrency.
- No physical address, no landline, no registration numbers.
- Stock photographs of the "team".
- A hotel that has never heard of your booking when you call to check. Call one.
- Reluctance to get on a video call.

## Questions worth asking

1. Who is my point of contact from today until I fly home, and will that change?
2. Which hotels exactly, and which room category at each?
3. Is GST included? Are entrance fees and guides included, and in which cities?
4. Who is my driver, what is the vehicle, and does it change between cities?
5. What happens if my flight is cancelled, or I fall ill mid-journey?
6. What are the cancellation terms, in writing?
7. How do I pay, and what protection does that give me?
8. May I speak to a guest who travelled with you last season?
9. What are your company registration numbers?

A good operator will answer all nine without fuss, and will probably have covered most of them before you thought to ask.
