Mysore was the capital of the Wodeyar maharajas for most of five hundred years, and we think it's the most pleasant city in South India because of the way they ran it. A ruler who took his job seriously laid out broad avenues and parks, a planned market, a university and a zoo in the early twentieth century, and the city still works on that plan. The palace in the middle, rebuilt in 1912 after a fire, mixes Hindu, Islamic and Gothic ideas, with stained-glass peacocks, cast-iron pillars from Glasgow and a durbar hall where the maharaja sat on a golden throne during Dasara. The family still lives in a wing at the back.

The city around it is for walking. The Devaraja Market has been in the same place since the 1880s and is the finest produce market we know in the south: a grid of covered lanes selling flowers by the kilo, bananas by the bunch and kumkum powder heaped in bright cones. Chamundi Hill rises about three hundred metres above the city to the south. At the top is a temple to the goddess who killed the buffalo demon Mahishasura, who gave Mysore its name, and there's a monolithic bull halfway up. The Wodeyars' industries are still running too, from the silk factory and the sandalwood oil distillery to the incense trade that made Mysore agarbathi known around the world.

Two nights covers the palace by day and by night, the market and the hill, and either Srirangapatna or a half day of crafts. Pattabhi Jois taught Ashtanga yoga here for sixty years, and students still come to the city for months at a time. For us Mysore is the hinge of Karnataka: three hours from Bengaluru, two and a half from the coffee hills of Coorg and two from the Kabini river and its leopards. If you can be here in late September or October, the ten days of Dasara, with the palace lit every night and an elephant procession on the last day, are worth building a whole journey around.
