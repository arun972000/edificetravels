The French arrived in 1674 and left in 1954, which is why Pondicherry has a rue Dumas and a rue Suffren, a war memorial to the Poilus, a lycée, policemen in red kepis and a bakery that opens at six with proper croissants. The town they built is a grid of yellow and ochre villas with tall louvred doors and gardens of frangipani, set behind a seafront promenade on the Bay of Bengal. Across a canal behind it is the Tamil town, which is older and in some ways finer, with streets of courtyard houses where families still sit out on their tiled verandahs in the evening.

The town changed in 1926, when the Bengali revolutionary and philosopher Sri Aurobindo founded an ashram here with a Frenchwoman, Mirra Alfassa, known as the Mother. The ashram now owns a third of the French Quarter, and its buildings are painted grey and kept quiet. In the main courtyard people sit in silence around a flower-covered tomb, and in the back streets the ashram runs a paper works, a bakery and a hand-loom unit. Ten kilometres north, Auroville, the Mother's experimental township, has been turning a plateau of eroded red earth into forest since 1968. Its golden Matrimandir stands in a clearing at the centre.

Two nights at an easy pace suits Pondicherry. Walk the grid in the morning. In the heat, swim in the hotel pool or drive out to Serenity Beach or the boat jetty at Chunnambar. Go to Auroville in the late afternoon, then join the evening promenade when the seafront road closes to traffic and the town comes out to eat ice cream and watch the waves hit the boulders. The food alone is worth the stop: Tamil fish curry and French pastry, and the Creole cooking that grew up between them. From here the great Chola-era temple at Chidambaram is ninety minutes south, with Thanjavur beyond it, and that's the way most of our Tamil Nadu journeys go.
