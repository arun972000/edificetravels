In 1960, a year after he fled Tibet, the Dalai Lama was offered a half-abandoned British hill station in the Kangra valley as a home, and he took it. More than sixty years later McLeod Ganj, the upper town of Dharamshala, is the capital of Tibet in exile. It has a monastery, a parliament, a library and schools, and a main street of momo stalls and prayer wheels strung along a ridge at 2,000 metres. Behind it the Dhauladhar range goes straight up like a wall. The peaks pass 4,500 metres within a few kilometres of the town, and in spring they're still white.

You can walk the town in a day. The Tsuglagkhang complex, the Dalai Lama's own temple and residence, is at the southern end, and when he's at home the courtyard fills before dawn. The Tibet Museum beside it doesn't spare you, and you should go. Below the main square, the old British church of St John in the Wilderness stands among the cedars, with the grave of a Viceroy in its yard. Further down, the Norbulingka Institute keeps Tibetan painting, carving and tailoring going in a garden of streams and bamboo. Forest paths lead out of town to the waterfall at Bhagsu, the village of Dharamkot and the long climb to Triund.

We give Dharamshala two or three nights, and we come to it from Amritsar, five hours below on the plains, so that you go from Punjab's wheat fields to the snow line in a single day. Most visitors miss the Kangra valley beneath the town, which has one of the oldest forts in India, temples cut from living rock, tea gardens and the painters' village of Andretta. From Dharamshala the road runs east to Shimla or north over the passes to Lahaul and Spiti, and our Himalayan journeys go both ways.
