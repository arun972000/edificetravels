India has more great buildings than anyone can see in one trip, and most of them are still in use. Mosques are still prayed in, palaces are still lived in, and the temples hold worship every day, which is why a heritage journey here never feels like a walk through a museum.

Most first journeys begin with the Mughals and the Rajputs. In Delhi, Humayun's Tomb shows you the garden tomb the Taj was modelled on, and the Taj itself, seen from across the river at dusk and from inside the gates at dawn, still does what photographs can't. Then west into Rajasthan, where every ridge has a fort and every fort tells a different story: Amber's mirrored halls, Mehrangarh on its cliff above the blue city, Jaisalmer's golden walls, which people still live inside, and Udaipur's white palaces on the lake.

Further south the buildings get older. The Chola temple at Thanjavur was finished in 1010 and still holds daily worship. Hampi was one of the largest cities in the world around 1500 and is now a landscape of granite boulders and temple ruins you can walk for days. At Mahabalipuram, Pallava sculptors carved whole temples out of single rocks by the sea.

## How we plan a heritage journey

Timing comes first. We know which gate opens when, which courtyards fill by ten, and which forts look best in the late afternoon, and we build the days around that. Then the guides, who make or break a monument. Then the hotels, because sleeping inside a haveli or a palace is part of the history, and some of India's best heritage is the hotel itself.

We also keep the pace humane. Two monuments in a day is plenty. The rest of the time goes on the things that make the history make sense: lunch in a village beside a stepwell, a block printer in Sanganer, dinner with a Rajput family who can tell you which of their ancestors built what.

## Who it suits

First-time visitors, mostly, and anyone who loves architecture, history or photography. Children over about eight tend to love the forts. If you have been before and seen the Golden Triangle, the temple towns of the south are the natural next journey.
