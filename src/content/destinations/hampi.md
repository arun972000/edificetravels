In 1500 Vijayanagara was one of the largest cities on earth, with perhaps half a million people, seven rings of walls and bazaars where Portuguese visitors saw rubies and diamonds sold in heaps. In 1565 its army lost a battle on the Deccan, the sultans of the north sacked the city for six months, and nobody ever lived there again. What's left is spread over twenty-six square kilometres of the Tungabhadra valley in northern Karnataka: temples and palaces, stables, tanks and aqueducts, among granite boulders the size of houses. Local legend says the monkey armies of the Ramayana threw them there.

The ruins fall into two parts. The sacred centre, along the river, has the great temples. Virupaksha is still in worship, and its elephant takes coins in her trunk. Vittala has the stone chariot and the pillars that ring like bells when you tap them. The royal centre, three kilometres south, has the Lotus Mahal, the domed elephant stables and the stepped tank, a geometric pool of green granite found buried and intact in the 1980s. The old bazaar streets run between the two, and above them are the hills, Matanga and Hemakuta, which you climb at dawn or dusk to see the whole city laid out among the boulders and banana groves.

Two nights is the minimum and three is right. Hampi is hot and spread out, so we work the mornings and the last two hours of light and leave the middle of the day for the pool. Anegundi, the village across the river, adds a day of cycling and a coracle crossing. Getting here takes some effort: six hours from Bengaluru by road, an overnight train, or a flight to Hubballi and a three-hour drive. Goa is five hours west, and Hampi is a good reason to leave the beach for three days.
