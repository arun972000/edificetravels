Varanasi isn't an easy city, and we'd never pretend otherwise. It's loud and crowded, and it puts death in front of you as nowhere else does. It's also the place our guests talk about most when they get home. Hindus have been coming here to die for three thousand years, because to be cremated at Manikarnika and have your ashes go into the Ganges is to be freed from rebirth. That belief gives the city a frankness about life that you'll find bracing once you stop flinching.

The ghats are why you come: eighty-odd stone stairways running seven kilometres along the west bank. The only way to see them properly is from a boat, and we go out twice. At dawn we row north from Assi Ghat as the sun lifts over the empty far shore, while the city washes, prays, shaves and does its laundry on the steps. At dusk the priests at Dashashwamedh Ghat swing their fire lamps over the river in the aarti, and a thousand people watch from the stairs. From the water you have the best seat and none of the crush.

Behind the ghats are the lanes, barely wider than your shoulders, where most of Varanasi actually lives and works. There are brocade weavers, brass sellers and sweet shops, cows that have right of way, and the gold spire of the Kashi Vishwanath temple above them. Give yourself two full days, one for the river and the lanes and one for Sarnath and the quieter temples in the south of the city. In winter, start one morning with malaiyo, a froth of saffron and pistachio whisked from milk left out overnight in the dew. It's gone from the stalls by nine.
