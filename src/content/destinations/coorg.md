Coorg is the coffee district of Karnataka, a range of forested hills on the Western Ghats between Mysore and the Kerala coast, and the people who live there would like you to call it Kodagu. It's one of the wettest places in India during the monsoon and one of the mildest for the rest of the year. That's why coffee grows here under native forest and silver oak, with pepper vines climbing the trees and cardamom in the shade. India is the sixth-largest coffee producer in the world, and about a third of it comes from these hills.

The Kodavas, the people of the district, are unlike anyone else in the south. They were farmers and soldiers. The British exempted them from the gun laws, and they are still allowed to own firearms without a licence. Two of the Indian army's most famous generals, Cariappa and Thimayya, were Kodavas. Their religion centres on their ancestors and the river Kaveri, the men's dress is a black wrap-around coat with a red-and-gold sash, and they eat pork, which sets them apart from most Hindu communities in the south. Pandi curry, dark with kachampuli vinegar and served with rice dumplings, is the dish, and the place to eat it is a family table in a homestay.

We send people to Coorg for two or three nights on a working estate, between the palace at Mysore and the Kerala coast or the Kabini river. The days are slow. You walk the estate in the morning, then drive out to the elephant camp at Dubare or the Tibetan monastery at Bylakuppe, or up to Talakaveri, the source of the Kaveri, if you want a view. At four there's coffee on the verandah, grown a few hundred metres away. The harvest, November to February, is when you see the work being done. Madikeri, the small hill-top capital, has a fort and a Sunday market, and otherwise it's a place you drive through on the way to a planter's chair.
