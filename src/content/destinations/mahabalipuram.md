Mahabalipuram, which Tamil Nadu now spells Mamallapuram, was the port of the Pallava dynasty in the seventh and eighth centuries, when their ships traded with Sri Lanka and south-east Asia and their kings decided to carve a hill into temples. They left cave shrines cut into a granite ridge, free-standing temples hewn from single boulders, one of the largest open-air reliefs in the world, and a stone temple on the beach that has stood against the Bay of Bengal for 1,300 years. It all sits in a small town with a fishing beach, a few lanes of guesthouses and the sound of chisels.

The Pallavas never finished most of it. The Five Rathas, a group of temples carved downwards from one outcrop in the shape of chariots, were abandoned before the final polish and still carry the carvers' marks. Several caves have one wall completed and the other barely begun, so you can see exactly how the work was done. It's still being done. The town has a sculpture college and hundreds of working stonemasons, and from early morning the lanes ring with hammers on granite. The gods and garden lions they carve go to temples across India and to gardens in California and Surrey.

One night is enough for the monuments. Take two if you want a morning on the beach, or a day at the Dakshinachitra heritage village and the Cholamandal artists' colony on the road to Chennai. Mahabalipuram works best as the first stop on a Tamil Nadu route: ninety minutes from the airport, slow and quiet, with the Shore Temple at dawn as your first sight of the south. From here the East Coast Road runs south through salt pans and casuarina groves to Pondicherry, two hours away, and on to the temple country of the Kaveri delta.
