Amritsar is a city built around a pool. The Harmandir Sahib, the Golden Temple, stands in the middle of a square tank of water in the middle of the old town, its upper storeys covered in gold leaf, and the whole of Sikhism turns around it. A hundred thousand people a day walk the marble causeway to pray, and afterwards the kitchen feeds them for nothing, whoever they are. Many of our guests say it moved them more than anywhere else in India, and most itineraries leave it out.

We include it, and the temple is only part of the reason. Amritsar holds the two great traumas of modern India within a few hundred metres of each other. Jallianwala Bagh is the walled garden where British troops fired on a peaceful crowd in 1919; the bullet marks are still in the brick. The Partition Museum, in the old Town Hall, tells the story of 1947, when the new border was drawn thirty kilometres from here and some fifteen million people crossed it. See both, then drive out to the Attari-Wagah border for the evening ceremony and watch the same line performed as theatre.

Then there's the food. Punjab is India's wheat bowl, and Amritsar is where the wheat becomes bread: kulcha, paratha and naan, each from its own tandoor, each with a dhaba that has been making it for decades. One night gives you the temple at dawn and the border at dusk. Two let you eat properly. Either way, this is where we begin Himalayan journeys, on the last of the plains, with the road to Dharamshala and the Kangra valley leaving from the edge of town.
