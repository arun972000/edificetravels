Jodhpur sits where the farmland gives out and the Thar Desert begins. Rao Jodha of the Rathore clan founded it in 1459, and it grew rich on the caravan trade between Delhi and Gujarat and spent the money on a fort. Mehrangarh rises 120 metres on a sheer outcrop above the town, and its walls grow out of the rock so cleanly that it's hard to see where one stops and the other starts. Rudyard Kipling called it the work of angels, fairies and giants.

The fort is the reason to come, and it deserves time. The museum inside is run by the royal family's own trust and is the best-kept in Rajasthan. It has cradles and palanquins, a tent captured from the Mughals, a room of turbans, and a gallery of miniatures where you can follow the Marwar style over three centuries. From the cannon on the ramparts the old city below is plainly blue, the colour of indigo wash on lime. On one side the view runs out to the desert, and on the other to the white marble cenotaphs of Jaswant Thada.

Down in the town, Jodhpur is a bazaar city. Sardar Market around the Clock Tower sells everything from saffron to steel buckets, and the blue houses are in the lanes behind it. Outside the walls, the Bishnoi villages to the south are the gentlest introduction to rural Rajasthan we know. The restored stepwell at Toorji ka Jhalra, with cafés around it, is where the city's young people spend their evenings. Two nights is right. That gives you the fort properly, a morning in the villages and a slow afternoon before the long drive west to Jaisalmer or south to Udaipur.
