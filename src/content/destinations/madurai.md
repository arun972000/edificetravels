Madurai is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, and it has always been a temple town. Greek and Roman traders knew it in the second century BC, Pandyan kings ruled from it for a thousand years, and in the 1600s the Nayaks rebuilt its great temple into roughly the form it has today. It's a walled rectangle of six hectares in the middle of the city, with fourteen gopuram towers up to fifty metres high, every one of them covered in painted gods.

The Meenakshi Amman Temple is dedicated to the goddess first and her husband Shiva second, which is unusual and tells you something about the city. Fifteen thousand people come to worship on an ordinary day and a hundred thousand at festivals, so expect crowds and noise. Shops selling bangles, brass and spices line the outer corridors. Pilgrims fill the steps of the Golden Lotus tank at dawn, and the Thousand Pillar Hall, every column cut from a single block of granite, now houses a museum of bronzes. Go at five in the morning, when the doors open and the first worshippers arrive barefoot in the half-dark, and again at nine at night for the ceremony when Shiva is carried to Meenakshi's bedroom. Midday is for the palace.

Thirumalai Nayak built that palace in 1636, and what stands today is about a quarter of the original. Its style mixes Dravidian building with the Islamic architecture of the Deccan: a courtyard of enormous white columns and a domed throne room that echoes like a cathedral. Away from the temple and palace, Madurai eats late. The streets around the temple serve parotta and salna, kari dosai with its layer of minced mutton, and sweet jigarthanda until after midnight. Two nights is right. Arrive in the afternoon for the night ceremony, see the temple again at dawn and the palace and markets by day, and leave the next morning for the Chettinad mansions, an hour and a half to the north-east.
