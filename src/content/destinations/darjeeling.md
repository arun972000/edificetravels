Darjeeling was acquired from the Kingdom of Sikkim in 1835 as a sanatorium for the East India Company's officers, and it's still a place to go and get better. The town hangs on a ridge at just over 2,000 metres, with tea gardens dropping away on every side. On a clear day the eastern Himalaya fills the northern horizon: Kanchenjunga, 8,586 metres, directly in front and close enough to make out the glaciers, and Everest a small white tooth far off to the west. The air smells of woodsmoke and pine, and the pace is somewhere between a hill town and a village.

Tea is the reason the town exists. The first bushes were planted here in the 1840s from Chinese seed, and the cool, misty slopes produced a light, flowery leaf that in 2004 became the first product in India to receive a Geographical Indication. Eighty-seven gardens still cover the hills. Several of the best, Makaibari, Glenburn, Happy Valley and Castleton among them, let visitors walk the rows, go round the factory and taste. The first flush, picked from mid-March, is the most prized. A spring day on an estate, with the pickers moving through the bushes and the factory running, is what we send guests here for.

The town itself takes half a day. Walk the Mall to Chowrasta, look in at the zoo for the red pandas, and give the mountaineering institute an hour, since Tenzing Norgay taught there. If the toy train is running, ride it round the Batasia Loop. Two or three nights is right, and the drive on to Sikkim or down to Bagdogra is about three hours either way. Come in the clear, cold months from October to December, or in spring, when the rhododendrons are out and the first flush is coming in.
