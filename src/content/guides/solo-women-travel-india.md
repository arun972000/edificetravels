Almost every first email we get from a woman planning to travel alone asks the same thing: is India safe for me? Yes, for most women most of the time, though it asks more of your attention than most countries you'll have visited. This guide is what we tell our own friends.

## What is actually different?

Three things. People will stare, people will ask you personal questions, and crowded public transport carries a real risk of groping. The first two are tiring rather than dangerous, and the third you can avoid.

**You will be looked at.** Staring isn't considered rude in much of India, and a foreign woman on her own draws more eyes than a couple does. It's rarely hostile. It is tiring.

**You will be asked questions.** Are you married? Where's your husband? Why are you alone? Women ask as often as men, and it's usually curiosity. A short answer and a smile ends it. Some travellers wear a plain ring and mention a husband back at the hotel. You don't have to, but it works.

**Crowds and public transport carry real risk.** Groping happens in packed buses, suburban trains and festival crowds. Serious incidents against foreign women are rare, and they cluster in the same places: crowded public transport, poorly lit streets late at night, and cheap rooms in the wrong part of town. Stay out of those and you've removed most of the exposure.

A great deal of India is no different from anywhere else. Heritage sites, good hotels, restaurants, markets by day and the countryside are places where women travel alone every day, millions of Indian women among them. The idea that the whole country is a threat is wrong, and it keeps people from coming.

## How does a private journey change the picture?

It takes away most of the risk described above. Those are the risks of being alone in public at the wrong time, and on a private journey you rarely are.

You keep one driver for the whole trip. We know his name, his licence, his family and how many years he has driven for us, and you'll have his photo and number before you land. He meets you at arrivals with a sign, carries your bags, waits while you eat and drives you back to the hotel after dinner, so you never have to find an auto-rickshaw at eleven at night. In each city a licensed guide walks with you through the places where being alone is the uncomfortable part, such as Old Delhi's lanes or the ghats of Varanasi.

Women drivers are available on request in Delhi and a handful of other cities through trained women-driver programmes. There aren't many of them and they're booked early, so tell us while we're planning the trip. We'll be straight with you about where we can arrange one and where we can't.

## What should I wear?

Loose clothes that cover your shoulders and knees. Nobody will make you, but it noticeably cuts down the attention you get. A long cotton kurta over trousers, or a maxi dress with a scarf, works everywhere. Buy a kurta on your first day in Delhi: it costs little, it suits the heat, and people respond to you differently in it. Carry a scarf for temples and mosques, where you may need to cover your head. Swimwear is normal at hotel pools and on Goa's beaches. Mumbai, Bengaluru and the smarter parts of Delhi are relaxed. Small-town Rajasthan, Varanasi and religious sites are more conservative.

## Are trains safe?

Yes, if you're in the right class at a sensible hour. Day trains such as the Shatabdi and Vande Bharat, in air-conditioned chair class, are comfortable and full of families and business travellers. For overnight journeys we book first or second class AC and ask for an upper berth, which is out of reach from the aisle. Delhi's metro reserves the first coach of every train for women, and it's an easy way to reach Old Delhi. We avoid arriving at or leaving a station after midnight, unreserved coaches and suburban trains at rush hour. Your driver will be waiting at the station exit when you arrive.

## Can I go out at night?

Yes. Use the judgement you would at home and add one rule: don't walk back. Dinner in a restaurant or bar in Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Jaipur or Goa is normal. Have your driver or the hotel car take you both ways, or in the big cities use Uber or Ola and share the trip status with someone. Go easy on the alcohol, because being visibly drunk draws attention that's hard to shake off. In smaller towns, eat at your hotel or a restaurant close by. It's the sensible choice, and often the best food in town.

## Hotels

When we choose hotels for a woman travelling alone, we look for a central location you can walk out of by day, a 24-hour reception, a well-lit entrance and managers we know. Family-run havelis in Rajasthan and homestays in Kerala are often the best choice of all, because the owners take a personal interest in who is staying under their roof. Ask for a room near the lift rather than at the end of a corridor. Use the chain, don't open the door to staff you weren't expecting, and ring reception if anything feels off. None of this is specific to India. It's still worth saying.
