Indians are famously forgiving of visitors who get the manners wrong. Nobody expects you to know everything, and a smile and an apology cover most mistakes. Still, a few things make a real difference to how you're received, and they're easy to learn before you go.

## Clothes

**Cover your shoulders and knees** in towns, villages and anywhere religious. Loose cotton is cooler than tight clothes anyway, and it keeps the sun off. Long trousers, long skirts, kurtas and linen shirts work everywhere.

**Resorts and cities are different.** At beach resorts in Goa and Kerala, at hotel pools and in the smarter parts of Mumbai, Delhi and Bengaluru, people dress much as they do in Europe. Swimwear belongs on the beach and at the pool, never on the street or in a village.

**Carry a scarf.** It covers your head in a gurdwara, your shoulders in a temple, and your face on a dusty road. Women find a light dupatta, the long scarf worn with a kurta, useful almost every day.

**Buy some clothes there.** A kurta or two from a good shop in Jaipur or Delhi costs little, fits the climate and is the easiest way to dress appropriately. Your guide will know where.

## Temples, gurdwaras and mosques

**Shoes off,** always, at the entrance to any temple, gurdwara or mosque, and often in homes. There's usually a shoe stand; give the attendant a small tip when you collect them. Stone floors get hot in the sun, so walk on the mats and go early.

**Hindu temples.** Walk around shrines clockwise. Don't touch images of the gods, and don't photograph inside the sanctum. A few temples, mostly in Kerala and including the Padmanabhaswamy temple in Thiruvananthapuram, admit only Hindus, and some of them ask men to remove their shirts and wear a mundu. Your guide will know.

**Jain temples.** Leave leather outside: belts, bags, watch straps and wallets. Some Jain temples, including Ranakpur, also ask that women who are menstruating don't enter. The rules are posted at the gate.

**Gurdwaras.** Everyone covers their head; the Golden Temple lends orange scarves at the entrance. Wash your feet in the shallow pool by the gate. No tobacco or alcohol anywhere near. In the langar, the free kitchen, sit on the floor in a row and take what you're given with both hands cupped.

**Mosques.** Visit outside prayer times, especially Friday midday. Women cover their heads, arms and legs, and some areas are for men only. At the Jama Masjid in Delhi, robes are available at the gate.

## Hands, feet and heads

**Use your right hand** to eat, to pass and receive things, and to give money. The left is traditionally used for washing.

**Mind your feet.** Feet are considered unclean, so don't point the soles at people or at shrines, don't step over someone sitting on the floor, and don't put your feet on furniture in someone's home. If you brush someone with your foot by accident, touching your hand to your chest is an apology.

**Leave heads alone.** Patting a child's head is common between family, but from a stranger it can feel odd. In Buddhist areas, avoid touching anyone's head at all.

## Greetings and conversation

**Namaste,** palms together and a slight nod, works with everyone and is always polite. Men often shake hands with other men. With women, wait for her to offer her hand.

**The head wobble** is a gentle side-to-side tilt that means yes, I'm listening, or all right, depending on context. You'll be doing it yourself within a week.

**Expect personal questions.** Are you married? How many children? What do you do? It's friendliness, not prying, and it's fine to ask the same in return. Politics, religion and relations with Pakistan are better left to people who know you well.

**Public affection** between couples, kissing especially, draws stares outside the big cities. Holding hands is fine. Friends of the same sex often hold hands too, and it means only friendship.

## Eating

In much of the south, and in many homes everywhere, people eat with their right hand. You're welcome to try; tear bread with the fingers of one hand and use it to scoop. Cutlery is always available if you ask.

In a home, you'll be offered more food than you can eat, and refusing once is expected. Leaving a little on the plate signals you've had enough. Wait to be shown where to sit, and wash your hands before and after.

## Money, markets and bargaining

Bargaining is normal in markets and with street sellers, and not done in shops with marked prices, malls or restaurants. Keep it friendly, start well below the first price and be ready to walk away. If you aren't sure, ask your guide, who will know the going rate. For tipping, see our guide to money, tipping and staying connected.

## A few words of Hindi

Most people you'll deal with speak some English, but a word or two of Hindi goes a long way in the north. Namaste for hello. Dhanyavaad, or the more casual shukriya, for thank you. Haan and nahin for yes and no. Bas, for enough, which is useful at dinner. In the south, Hindi is less spoken, and a simple thank you in English is perfectly polite.
