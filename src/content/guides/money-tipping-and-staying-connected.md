In India you'll see a QR code taped to every fruit cart and temple donation box, and far less cash changing hands than you might expect. The payment system behind those codes is mostly closed to foreign visitors, though, so money works a little differently for you than for the people around you.

## Cash, cards or UPI?

Use a card for hotels and larger bills, and cash for everything small. UPI, the system most Indians pay with, needs an Indian bank account, so it's largely out of your reach.

**UPI** is the QR-code system behind all those stickers, and your home banking app can't join it. A prepaid wallet called UPI One World is sold to visitors from G20 countries at some international airports against a passport and visa, and you load it with cash or card. Availability is patchy, and in practice most of our guests travel without it.

**Cards** work where you'd expect: hotels, better restaurants, larger shops, airline and train tickets online, and a growing number of monument ticket counters. Visa and Mastercard are accepted almost everywhere cards are taken; American Express much less so. If a terminal offers to charge you in your home currency, decline and pay in rupees, because the machine's rate is always worse than your bank's. Tell your bank you're travelling, and carry two cards from different accounts.

**Cash** covers the rest: auto-rickshaws, tips, markets, small restaurants, roadside tea and the temple shoe-keepers. Notes in circulation are INR 500, 200, 100, 50, 20 and 10. The INR 2,000 note was withdrawn in 2023, so decline one if it's offered. The everyday difficulty is finding small notes. Break your INR 500s at hotels and larger shops, and keep the 100s and 200s for tips.

Most couples find that INR 3,000 to 5,000 a day in cash covers lunches, tips, autos and small purchases, with the card doing the rest.

## ATMs

Withdraw rupees when you land. You can't buy them at a sensible rate before you fly. Airport ATMs are fine for a first INR 10,000, and after that your driver will take you to an ATM at a bank branch, which is safer than a standalone machine and more likely to have cash. State Bank of India, HDFC, ICICI and Axis machines all take foreign cards. The limit per transaction is usually INR 10,000, sometimes 20,000, and the Indian bank charges around INR 200 to 250 on top of whatever your own bank takes, so fewer, larger withdrawals cost less. If a machine keeps your card, which is rare, note the bank and the time and message us straight away.

If you bring foreign cash, US dollars, euros and pounds change easily at authorised dealers in every city. Hotel exchange desks are convenient and poor value. Keep the receipt.

## How much should I tip?

Around INR 500 to 800 a day for your driver and INR 500 to 1,000 a day for a local guide, in cash. Those amounts are for your whole party; nobody expects one from each person. Tips are expected in Indian tourism and they matter. We pay our drivers and guides fairly, and tips are still a real part of their income. Give them in rupees, directly to the person. These are the figures we suggest to our own guests.

| Who | How much | When |
|---|---|---|
| Driver | INR 500 to 800 per day | In one envelope at the end of the journey |
| Local guide | INR 500 to 1,000 per day | At the end of their day or days with you |
| Hotel porter | INR 100 | On arrival and departure |
| Restaurants | 5 to 10% | Only where the bill shows no service charge |
| Hotel housekeeping | INR 100 a day | Optional, left on your last morning |

You don't need to tip in a temple, at a monument or anyone who hasn't done something for you. A firm, polite no is enough.

## SIM or eSIM?

If you want an Indian phone number, buy a tourist SIM or eSIM from Airtel or Jio at the airport. If data is all you need, an international eSIM such as Airalo or Holafly, installed before you fly, is simpler. An Indian number is handy because one-time passcodes for hotel Wi-Fi, train bookings and some payment terminals go to an Indian mobile, and because your driver and guide can reach you easily.

**Tourist SIM at the airport.** Airtel and Jio have counters in the arrivals hall at Delhi, Mumbai and the other main international airports. Bring your passport and visa and allow a few minutes; they take your photograph on the spot. Activation can take some hours, so don't worry if it isn't working by the time you reach the car.

**Tourist eSIM.** Airtel and Jio also sell eSIMs for compatible phones, against the same documents.

**International eSIM before you fly.** Airalo and Holafly both cover India. Install it at home, land connected and skip the counter. These are data-only, so you get no Indian number and passcodes sent by SMS won't reach you. Plenty of our guests pair one with hotel Wi-Fi and manage perfectly well.

Coverage is strong in every city and along the main roads. It thins out in the deep desert, the high Himalaya and parts of the Northeast, and your driver will tell you where.

## WhatsApp is how India talks

Your driver, your guide, your hotel's front desk and your specialist all use WhatsApp, and you will too. You might send your driver a voice note about tomorrow's pick-up, send your specialist a photo of a menu asking which dish to order, or share your live location on a long drive so someone at home can watch the dot move. We run the whole journey on one WhatsApp line because that's how things work here, and because a message at 6 am asking whether the fort is open gets a real answer from someone in the same time zone.

One last thing: split your cash, cards and passport between two places, so a lost wallet on day two doesn't spoil the next three weeks.
