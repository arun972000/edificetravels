Ladakh looks nothing like the India most people expect. It's a cold desert on the far side of the Himalaya, closer in feel to Tibet than to the plains, where the Indus runs through brown valleys below six-thousand-metre peaks and the sky is a hard, dark blue. Buddhism arrived in the eighth century and stayed. The monasteries on their crags, Thiksey, Hemis, Likir and Lamayuru, are still full of monks, and the villages below them grow barley and apricots in the four months when the ground isn't frozen. For the other eight the passes close and Ladakh is on its own.

That short season is why planning matters. The road from Manali opens in June and shuts in October. Flights run all year, but they only land in the morning and only when the wind allows. We fly you in and then do nothing with you for a day and a half, because Leh is at 3,500 metres and altitude sickness doesn't care how fit you are. Walk to the old town, drink tea, sleep. The journeys start on day three: the Sham valley monasteries along the Indus, then over the Khardung La to Nubra, then east over the Chang La to Pangong Tso. Every one of those passes is higher than Mont Blanc.

A week is the minimum that makes sense, and ten days is better. The distances are long and the roads slow, and each of these places deserves a night of its own. We use family-run hotels in Leh, village guesthouses in the valleys and comfortable camps at the lakes, with Ladakhi drivers who know which bend the army checkpoint is on. In winter, for the determined, there are snow leopards in Hemis National Park and the frozen Zanskar river to walk on. For most guests, though, Ladakh is a journey for June to September, when the barley is green and the monastery festivals are on, while the rest of India sits under the monsoon.
