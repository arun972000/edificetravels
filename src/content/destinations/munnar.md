Munnar is tea. The road climbs out of the humid Kerala lowlands through rubber and pineapple, then cardamom under tall shade trees, and at around 1,400 metres the hills turn into the green corduroy of tea bushes running up to every ridge. The British planted the first estates here in the 1880s, cutting a road up from the plains and bringing Tamil labourers to work them. The Kanan Devan company still runs most of the land. The pluckers' children live in the lines their great-grandparents built, and in season the factories roll and dry leaf through the night.

The town is small and unlovely, a junction of three rivers with a bus stand and a market. You want to be outside it, in a planter's bungalow on an estate, with a verandah facing the hills and a fire lit after dinner. From there you walk. The estate roads are quiet and the pluckers will wave you over to watch, and each bend opens onto another valley. Eravikulam National Park, fifteen kilometres north, protects the high grasslands under Anamudi, and its Nilgiri tahr are so used to visitors that they graze a few metres from the path.

Two nights is the right stay. One full day covers Eravikulam in the morning and a tea factory in the afternoon, with a stop at the Tea Museum if you like old machinery. A second morning gives you Top Station, forty kilometres east, for the view across the Western Ghats into Tamil Nadu, or a walk to a waterfall on the estate. From Munnar most journeys drop south to Thekkady, about three and a half hours away, for the spice hills and Periyar, and from there to the backwaters.
