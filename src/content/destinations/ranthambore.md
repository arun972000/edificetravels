Ranthambore is where most first-time visitors see their first wild tiger, and there are good reasons for that. The forest is dry and open, with thin undergrowth for most of the season. The tigers have had forty years to get used to jeeps, and the lakes at the heart of the park draw them out to drink. On top of that there's a tenth-century fort on the skyline, temples with banyan roots growing through them, and sambar deer standing knee-deep in the water at dawn.

Practicalities matter more here than anywhere else we send you. The park is divided into ten zones. The lakes and the oldest tiger territories are in zones two to five, and permits for those sell out fast. Each safari lasts about three and a half hours, one at first light and one in the afternoon. In winter, one sighting in four drives is a fair expectation, and the odds improve in the heat of April. We use six-seater jeeps with naturalists we've worked with for years, and we book two nights as a minimum, because so often it's the second morning that works.

Between drives you rest at the lodge, but there's more here than tigers. The fort is open to anyone and takes a slow half-day. The Chambal River, an hour south, has gharials and dolphins and a stillness the park never has. The craft cooperative at Dastkar shows what conservation owes to the villages that were moved. Ranthambore is three hours from Jaipur and five from Agra, and Sawai Madhopur station is on the main Delhi to Mumbai line, so it fits into almost any northern journey. After a week of forts and cities, two or three days in the forest slow everything down.
