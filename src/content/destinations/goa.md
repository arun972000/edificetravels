Goa is the smallest state in India, and it was Portuguese for four and a half centuries, until 1961: more than twice as long as the British ruled the rest of the country. It shows. The villages have white churches with baroque façades and a cross at every junction. The houses have red tiles, deep verandahs and Portuguese names on the gateposts. People are called Fernandes and D'Souza, eat pork and drink feni distilled from cashew fruit, and a good number of them hold Portuguese passports. Behind the coast are paddy fields, coconut and cashew groves, laterite hills and slow rivers, with the Western Ghats rising twenty kilometres inland.

The beaches run for 100 kilometres, and they vary a great deal. The middle of the north coast, Calangute, Baga and Anjuna, is where the charter flights unload and the nightclubs are, and we leave it alone. North of the Chapora river, Morjim, Ashwem and Mandrem are long and quiet, lined with small hotels and good restaurants, and Arambol at the top is where the yoga crowd goes. In the far south, Agonda, Palolem and Patnem are fishing beaches backed by coconut palms, where the hotels are huts and guesthouses and dinner is by candlelight. In between, Panjim, the capital, has its old Latin quarter, and the churches of Old Goa are twenty minutes away.

We treat Goa as a place to rest for three or four nights, usually at the end of a journey: one day for Panjim, Old Goa and the inland villages, and the rest on whichever beach suits you. The food alone would justify it. Fish curry and rice at a lunch house, prawns on the beach at sunset, pork vindaloo made the way the Portuguese taught, bebinca for pudding. Goa is also the easy way into Hampi, five hours east across the Deccan, and we often pair the two for a week, with the beach at one end and the ruins of Vijayanagara at the other.
