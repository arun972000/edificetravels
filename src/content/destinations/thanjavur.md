Thanjavur sits in the delta of the Kaveri river, the flat green country of paddy and coconut that Tamils call the rice bowl. It was the capital of the Chola empire when that empire ran from Sri Lanka to the Ganges and sent fleets to Sumatra. In 1010 the emperor Rajaraja finished a temple here that was the largest in India at the time. Its tower rises sixty-six metres to a granite capstone said to weigh eighty tonnes, the Nandi bull in the courtyard is carved from a single block, and you can walk the courtyard for an hour before the detail starts to come into focus. The Brihadeeswarar, which everyone calls the Big Temple, is still in daily use, and the bells still ring at six.

After the Cholas came the Nayaks and then the Marathas, who ruled from a palace in the town until the British pensioned them off in 1855. The palace is shabby and wonderful. It has a durbar hall painted with Maratha kings, a seven-storey tower, and an art gallery of Chola bronzes that would be the centrepiece of any museum in Europe. Its library holds sixty thousand palm-leaf manuscripts collected by Serfoji II, a raja who corresponded with European scientists and ran a printing press in Devanagari. In the lanes around the palace live the painters who make Thanjavur's gold-leaf pictures and the craftsmen who build the veena.

Two nights gives you the Big Temple at dawn and again at dusk, the palace by day, and a full day for the sister temples at Darasuram and Gangaikonda Cholapuram and the bronze casters at Swamimalai. The delta is worth the drive for its own sake, with canals, rice at every stage of growth, and village temples with painted horses at their gates. Thanjavur sits at the centre of a Tamil temple route that runs from Mahabalipuram and Pondicherry in the north, through Chidambaram and Kumbakonam, and on to Chettinad and Madurai in the south.
