For many of our guests the food is the reason to come, and for a few it's the reason they nearly didn't. We like to put at least one meal worth a detour into every itinerary. This guide is about eating that meal, and the two hundred around it, without losing a day to your stomach.

## Why do people get ill, and how do you avoid it?

Usually from water they didn't realise they were drinking, in ice, washed salad or rinsed fruit. Food left standing in the heat, unwashed hands and simply eating more rich food than usual in 35°C weather account for most of the rest. Each has a simple answer.

- **Water.** Tap water isn't safe to drink anywhere in India, good hotels included. Drink bottled water with an intact seal, or filtered water from the dispensers most good hotels now provide for refilling a bottle. In small-town hotels, brush your teeth with it too.
- **Ice.** Fine in upmarket hotels and restaurants, where it's made from filtered water. Skip it at street stalls and small cafes.
- **Raw food.** In modest places, avoid salads, pre-cut fruit and chutneys that have been sitting out. Fruit you peel yourself is always fine.
- **Hands.** Sanitiser before every meal. It sounds obvious, and it makes the biggest single difference.
- **Pace.** Eat lightly for the first two days while your system adjusts, and go easy on fried food and cream-based gravies in the heat.

Do these and most people eat everything for three weeks with nothing worse than one day of feeling delicate.

## Can I eat street food?

Yes, and you should, as long as you follow three rules. Eat where it's busy, because quick turnover keeps the food fresh. Choose things cooked hot in front of you: parathas off the griddle, kebabs off the coals, samosas out of the oil, dosas off the tawa. And take care with chaat, the cold plates of yoghurt, chutney and crisp pastry. It's delicious and it's the riskiest category, so have it at an established shop with a crowd and leave the carts alone.

Start with a guided food walk on your first or second evening. In Old Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata and Chennai we work with guides who know which of the forty stalls in a lane have fed their own families for decades. After one walk you'll start spotting the good ones yourself.

## How spicy is it really?

Milder than its reputation in hotels and tourist restaurants, which tone everything down, and properly hot in some local kitchens. In a local restaurant in the north, ask for "less spicy" or "kam mirch" and you'll be understood. Spice here means flavour as much as chilli. A Mughlai korma in Delhi is rich and mild, a Rajasthani laal maas or an Andhra fish curry is seriously hot, and Kerala's coconut-based curries sit in between. Yoghurt, rice and bread cool your mouth. Water doesn't. Carry antacids. Within a week your palate adjusts, and plenty of guests start asking for the hotter version.

## Dishes not to miss

**In the north:** parathas from the lanes of Old Delhi for breakfast; butter chicken in the city that invented it; chole bhature in Punjab; galouti kebabs in Lucknow; laal maas and dal baati churma in Rajasthan; Amritsari kulcha with chana; rogan josh if you reach Kashmir; and a glass of lassi almost anywhere.

**In the south:** a sadya, the vegetarian feast on a banana leaf, in Kerala; appam with stew for breakfast; Malabar fish curry and karimeen pollichathu, pearl spot fish grilled in a banana leaf; Chettinad pepper chicken in Tamil Nadu; a Mysore masala dosa; Hyderabadi biryani; and filter coffee poured between two tumblers until it froths.

Tell your specialist which of these you want and we'll plan the day around the right kitchen.

## Vegetarian and vegan India

India is the easiest country in the world for vegetarians. A large share of the population never eats meat, every menu has a vegetarian section as long as the rest, and "pure veg" restaurants, with no meat in the kitchen at all, are everywhere. Packaged food carries a green dot for vegetarian and a brown one for non-vegetarian. Gujarat, Rajasthan and much of Tamil Nadu are vegetarian strongholds. Eggs count as non-vegetarian.

Vegans have a slightly harder time, simply because dairy turns up everywhere: ghee in the dal, paneer in the curry, yoghurt on the side. Say "no ghee, no paneer, no dahi, no makhan" and most kitchens will cook with oil instead. South Indian food, built on rice, lentils and coconut, is naturally closer to vegan and the easiest place to eat.

## Allergies and dietary needs

Nut allergies need care, because cashew paste thickens many northern gravies and peanuts turn up in chaat and chutneys. Coeliacs do well in the south, where rice replaces wheat, and need to avoid roti, naan and paratha in the north. Lactose intolerance is manageable with the same vocabulary vegans use. Halal food is widely available; kosher is rare outside Mumbai.

Tell us before you travel. We brief every hotel, guide and houseboat cook in writing, in the local language, and give you a printed card to show in restaurants. It works far better than explaining at the table.

## Pharmacies, doctors and insurance

Pharmacies are everywhere in cities and towns, well stocked and inexpensive. Apollo and MedPlus are the chains you'll see most. Bring your prescriptions with the generic drug names, because brand names differ. For a stomach upset, take oral rehydration salts (sold as Electral at any pharmacy), rest and eat plain food. Antibiotics are sold over the counter, but we wouldn't self-prescribe. If you're no better in two days, your hotel will call a doctor, usually within the hour.

Every major city has private hospitals of international standard. You need travel insurance that covers hospital treatment and medical evacuation, and we ask for your insurer's emergency number before you fly. See a travel clinic six to eight weeks before departure for vaccinations; hepatitis A and typhoid are routinely recommended. We plan journeys for a living, so on anything medical, take the clinic's advice over ours.
