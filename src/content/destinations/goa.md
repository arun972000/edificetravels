faqs:
  - q: "North Goa or South Goa?"
    a: "It depends what you want from the days. The north, above the Chapora river and in the inland villages, has the better restaurants, the old Portuguese houses and an easier run to Panjim and Old Goa. The south has the emptier beaches and the big resorts, and is quieter at night. We avoid the middle of the north coast, Baga, Calangute and Anjuna, which is crowded and loud."
  - q: "Is the sea safe for swimming?"
    a: "From November to April, on most beaches, yes, within the flagged areas. Lifeguards watch the main beaches and put up red flags where the currents are strong. Take them seriously, and don't swim after drinking or after dark. During the monsoon, from June to September, swimming is banned and the sea is dangerous."
  - q: "What should we wear?"
    a: "Swimwear is fine on the beach and by the pool, though nude and topless bathing are against the law. In the villages, the markets and Panjim, cover up a little more, and for churches and temples, cover shoulders and knees. A light scarf in your bag solves most of it."
  - q: "Can we see St Francis Xavier's body?"
    a: "His silver casket rests high on the tomb in the basilica, where you can see it from below but not look inside. Every ten years or so the relics are brought down and carried to the Sé Cathedral for an exposition of several weeks, which draws pilgrims from all over India. The last one was over the winter of 2024 to 2025."
  - q: "Is Goa very busy at Christmas?"
    a: "Very. From about 20 December to the first days of January, hotels are full, the coast roads jam and the northern beaches are crowded. It's also when Goa is at its most Catholic, with paper stars in the windows and midnight mass in every village. If you want it, book a year ahead; if not, come in January or February."

Goa is the smallest state in India, and it was Portuguese for four and a half centuries, until 1961: more than twice as long as the British ruled the rest of the country. It shows. The villages have white churches with baroque façades and a cross at every junction. The houses have red tiles, deep verandahs and Portuguese names on the gateposts. People are called Fernandes and D'Souza, eat pork and drink feni distilled from cashew fruit, and a good number of them hold Portuguese passports. Behind the coast are paddy fields, coconut and cashew groves, laterite hills and slow rivers, with the Western Ghats rising twenty kilometres inland.

The beaches run for 100 kilometres, and they vary a great deal. The middle of the north coast, Calangute, Baga and Anjuna, is where the charter flights unload and the nightclubs are, and we leave it alone. North of the Chapora river, Morjim, Ashwem and Mandrem are long and quiet, lined with small hotels and good restaurants, and Arambol at the top is where the yoga crowd goes. In the far south, Agonda, Palolem and Patnem are fishing beaches backed by coconut palms, where the hotels are huts and guesthouses and dinner is by candlelight. In between, Panjim, the capital, has its old Latin quarter, and the churches of Old Goa are twenty minutes away.

We treat Goa as a place to rest for three or four nights, usually at the end of a journey: one day for Panjim, Old Goa and the inland villages, and the rest on whichever beach suits you. The food alone would justify it. Fish curry and rice at a lunch house, prawns on the beach at sunset, pork vindaloo made the way the Portuguese taught, bebinca for pudding. Goa is also the easy way into Hampi, six hours east across the Deccan, and we often pair the two for a week, with the beach at one end and the ruins of Vijayanagara at the other.
