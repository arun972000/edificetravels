Kochi is where most people begin Kerala, and it's a gentle way in. The old port of Fort Kochi sits on a peninsula at the mouth of the harbour, a grid of low Portuguese and Dutch houses under rain trees, with the Arabian Sea on one side and a working harbour on the other. Vasco da Gama died here in 1524 and was buried in St Francis Church, which still stands. The Dutch took the town in 1663 and the British in 1795. Each left something behind: a palace, a cemetery, a street of warehouses, and a bakery that still makes bread the way the Portuguese taught.

Everyone comes for the Chinese fishing nets, a row of cantilevered timber frames on the fort's northern shore, each needing four or five men to lower and raise it. Go at half past five in the evening, when the crews are working and the light is behind the nets. Then walk south along the shore road past the Dutch Cemetery to the church, and east through Jew Town to Mattancherry. The synagogue there was built in 1568. The Dutch palace next door has some of the finest murals in India, painted for a raja who paid his debts to Portugal in pepper.

Two nights is right. That gives you a full day for the fort and Mattancherry, an evening of Kathakali, and a morning for the spice market or a harbour ferry before you drive east into the hills. Stay inside the fort, in one of the old merchant houses that are now small hotels, and you'll have the streets to yourself from six until the day-trippers arrive at ten. Eat well while you're here. Kochi has Syrian Christian fish curries, Mappila biryani and soft, lacy appams with stew for breakfast.
