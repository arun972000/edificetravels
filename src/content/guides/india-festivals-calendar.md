India keeps a festival calendar so full that something is being celebrated somewhere almost every week. A few are worth building a whole journey around. Most are simply a lucky extra if your dates happen to line up, and a good specialist will tell you when they do.

Two things to know first. Most Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist and Jain festivals follow the lunar calendar, so they move by up to a month from one year to the next. We give the usual month below and confirm exact dates when you enquire. And the big festivals fill hotels months ahead, so if one matters to you, tell us early.

## January

**Pongal and Makar Sankranti, mid-January.** The harvest festival. In Tamil Nadu, families cook sweet rice in new clay pots until it boils over, and decorate the cows. In Jaipur and across Gujarat, the sky fills with kites, and the rooftops of the old city are the place to be.

**Republic Day, 26 January.** The parade down Kartavya Path in Delhi, with regiments, camels and floats from every state. Seats are by ticket and security is tight; three days later, the Beating Retreat ceremony at dusk is quieter and, we think, more moving.

**Jaipur Literature Festival, late January.** Five days of talks that draw writers from around the world, and the city is busy all week.

## February

**Desert Festival, Jaisalmer, January or February.** Three days of camel races, turban-tying contests and folk music on the dunes at Sam, timed to the full moon.

**Losar, the Tibetan new year, February or March.** Celebrated in Dharamshala and the Tibetan monasteries, with masked dances and butter-lamp offerings. Ladakh keeps its own Losar in December or January.

**Maha Shivaratri, February or March.** A night of fasting and prayer to Shiva, at its most intense in Varanasi and at the great Shiva temples of the south.

## March

**Holi, February or March.** The festival of colour. A bonfire on the eve, then a morning of coloured powder and water that leaves whole towns pink and green. Mathura, Vrindavan and Barsana celebrate for a week, and Barsana's Lathmar Holi, where women chase the men with sticks, falls a few days before. It's riotous, joyful and, by early afternoon, over.

**Gangaur, March or April.** Rajasthan's women pray to the goddess Gauri for good husbands and long marriages, with processions through Jaipur and Udaipur.

## April and May

**Baisakhi, 13 or 14 April.** The Punjabi harvest festival and the Sikh new year, at its best at the Golden Temple in Amritsar.

**Thrissur Pooram, April or May.** Kerala's greatest temple festival: rows of caparisoned elephants, a percussion orchestra that plays for hours and a fireworks display that runs past midnight. It's very crowded; we find you a good place to stand.

**Buddha Purnima, April or May.** The Buddha's birthday, marked quietly at Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and the monasteries of Ladakh and Sikkim.

## June and July

**Hemis festival, Ladakh, June or July.** Two days of masked cham dances in the courtyard of Ladakh's largest monastery, with the great thangka unrolled every twelve years. It falls in the best season for Ladakh anyway.

**Rath Yatra, Puri, June or July.** Lord Jagannath and his siblings are pulled through the streets on vast wooden chariots by hundreds of thousands of pilgrims.

## August and September

**Nehru Trophy Boat Race, the second Saturday of August.** Snake boats more than thirty metres long, each with a hundred rowers, race on Punnamada Lake near Alleppey, in the middle of the monsoon.

**Onam, August or September.** Kerala's harvest festival: flower carpets outside every house, the great vegetarian feast of the sadya served on banana leaves, and more boat races.

**Ganesh Chaturthi, August or September.** Ten days for the elephant-headed god, ending with immersions of his images in the sea and rivers, at their largest in Mumbai.

## October

**Durga Puja, September or October.** Kolkata's great festival and, for many, the best street festival in India. Thousands of temporary pandals, some of them works of art, each with its image of the goddess, open to everyone until late at night.

**Dussehra, September or October.** The end of the nine nights of Navratri. Mysore celebrates with its Dasara: the palace lit by nearly a hundred thousand bulbs and a procession of elephants on the last day. In the Kullu valley the gods of the surrounding villages are carried down to the town in palanquins.

## November

**Diwali, October or November.** The festival of lights. Clay lamps on every windowsill, sweets exchanged between families, fireworks for three nights. Jaipur's bazaars are lit from end to end, and it's a beautiful time to be anywhere in Rajasthan. Many shops and some sights close on the day itself.

**Pushkar fair, October or November.** Thousands of camels, horses and cattle are traded on the dunes outside the small holy town, with pilgrims bathing in the lake as the full moon of Kartik approaches. Come for the first days, when the trading is real; the last days are for pilgrims.

**Dev Deepawali, Varanasi, fifteen days after Diwali.** Every ghat on the Ganges is lined with clay lamps, a sight people travel across India to see. Riverside rooms book out many months ahead.

**Guru Nanak Jayanti, the same full moon.** The birthday of the first Sikh Guru, when the Golden Temple is lit and the langar feeds even more people than usual.

## December

**Hornbill Festival, 1 to 10 December.** Nagaland's tribes gather at Kisama, near Kohima, for ten days of dance, song, wrestling and food. The only time of year many visitors make it to the far northeast.

**Christmas.** Midnight mass in Goa's old churches and in Fort Kochi, and Delhi's cathedral full to the doors.
