Shimla was built by people who were homesick. From 1864 to 1939 the entire Government of India moved up here every summer to escape the heat of Calcutta and later Delhi, and the town they built along the ridge is still half-timbered, gabled and Gothic. There's Christ Church on the Ridge, the Gaiety Theatre on the Mall, the post office and the library, and the Viceregal Lodge on its own hill, looking like a Scottish baronial castle that took a wrong turn. It's an odd place, and since there are no cars on the Mall, one of the most pleasant towns in India to walk.

The Mall runs along the top of the ridge for two kilometres, past the old shops and the Indian Coffee House. Every evening families, honeymooners and schoolchildren stroll up and down it, as they have for a century. The Ridge opens out beside it, with the church and the view north to the snow. Above, the deodar forest climbs to the Jakhu temple and its 33-metre statue of Hanuman. Below, the bazaars tumble down the south face of the hill, and that's where Shimla's own people live and shop. Two days gives you the town, a walk in the forest and the Viceregal Lodge.

Getting here is half the pleasure. The Kalka-Shimla narrow-gauge railway, a World Heritage Site since 2008, climbs from the plains in five slow hours of tunnels, viaducts and pine, and we use it for the way up and the car for the way down. Beyond Shimla the road goes on to the Kinnaur valley and Spiti, so it's on our high Himalayan routes as the last town of any size before the big mountains. For guests who want quiet, we stay fifteen kilometres out in Mashobra among the apple orchards and come into town for the evening.
