Kolkata was the capital of British India for nearly a hundred and forty years, and it has never quite stopped being a capital of something: Bengali literature and film, political argument, sweets, football. It's poorer and more crumbling than Delhi or Mumbai, and many of our most well-travelled guests say it's the Indian city they'd go back to first. Nobody is indifferent to it.

The centre still belongs to the Raj. The white marble Victoria Memorial stands on the Maidan, and nearby are the Writers' Building, St John's Church with Job Charnock's tomb, and the old clubs with their lawns and their sixty-year-old waiters. North of the centre is the old Bengali city of narrow lanes, mansions and sweet shops, where the Tagore family lived and the Durga idols are made. To the south are the leafy avenues of Ballygunge and Alipore, which is where the food is. The Hooghly runs past all of it. The Howrah Bridge across it carries more people on foot than almost any bridge on earth, and the flower market sits under its eastern end.

We give Kolkata two or three nights and plan it around the mornings, which are the city's best hours: the flower market and the bridge at six, a Chinese breakfast in Tiretta Bazaar at seven, and the College Street book stalls as they open. Afternoons are for museums and a long lunch, and the Maidan at dusk. If you can come during Durga Puja in October, do. For five nights the whole city turns into an open-air art show. From Kolkata it's an hour's flight north to Bagdogra for Darjeeling, or east to Guwahati for Assam.
